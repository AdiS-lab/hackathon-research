# 01: Evidence behind the ICP

Earlier evidence (timing science, Roberts/Kendrick/Bögels, alexia, hemiparesis, prior art) is in `../cue/01-evidence-ranked.md` and not repeated here except where the ICP depends on it.

## A. Who Ray is (clinical profile)

| # | Claim | Source | Conf. | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Aphasia affects about **21–38% of stroke survivors** (34–38% in some series). | Return-to-work review (Frontiers Rehab Sci 2025); StatPearls | HIGH | Population |
| A2 | Broca's aphasia: **comprehension usually relatively intact, so patients have insight, which leads to frustration**; "patients often feel they know what they wish to say but are unable to produce the words." 10–15% of acute first strokes; common in the chronic phase as global aphasia improves. | StatPearls (NBK436010); chronic-phase type distribution tables (EJNPN 2019) | HIGH (clinical) / MED (chronic %s vary by sample) | Ray's profile; why he's frustrated, not confused |
| A3 | **Broca's aphasia: verbs are harder than nouns**; agrammatism = short sentences, dropped function words, fewer verbs. | Chen & Bates (Aphasiology); noun–verb dissociation literature (SISSA review) | HIGH | Core-word buttons = verbs; camera = nouns; LLM = grammar |
| A4 | About **68% of people with chronic aphasia have central alexia**. | Brookshire et al. 2014, *Aphasiology* | MED-HIGH | No reading required; audio preview |
| A5 | **About 61% of people with post-stroke aphasia have right-side weakness** (74% with limb weakness, 82% of that right-sided). | EJNPN 2019 cohort | MED | Left-hand ring, one-handed UI |
| A6 | **Garrett & Lasker (2005)**: people with aphasia split into **"independent communicators"** (can use learned strategies and AAC on their own) and **"partner-dependent communicators"** (need a partner to cue them). | AAC-Aphasia Categories of Communicators; construct-validity study (aphasiology.pitt.edu 2137) | HIGH (standard clinical framework) | Ray = independent communicator; global aphasia = not our user |
| A7 | **People with aphasia spoke ~70% of the time even with an AAC device available.** | Linguistic analysis of three people with no prior AAC experience (CORE 78506497) | MED (n = 3) | "His voice beats Cue's voice": cancel queue when he speaks |

## B. Severity: what aphasia costs him

| # | Claim | Source | Conf. | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | **2M+ Americans** live with aphasia; ~180k new cases a year; more common than Parkinson's. | NAA; NIDCD; U-M | MED-HIGH | Headline number |
| B2 | **84.5% (2016) and 86.2% (2020) of Americans have never heard the term "aphasia."** Only ~7–9% can identify it as a language disorder. | National Aphasia Association awareness surveys (n = 1,142 in 2016) | MED-HIGH | Why people assume he's confused; "the biggest condition you've never heard of" |
| B3 | People with aphasia report **worse health-related QoL than people with cancer or Alzheimer's**; **up to 62% depressed** at 12 months post-stroke. | Lam & Wodchis 2010; Hilari et al.; Frontiers Comm 2023 review | MED-HIGH | Stakes |
| B4 | **Friendship loss.** Reasons for losing friends after stroke include aphasia and "unhelpful responses of others"; **people with aphasia experienced the most hurtful negative responses** and kept friends only with strong prior friendship patterns. After stroke, the **friends** part of the network declines while children/relatives stay stable. | Northcott & Hilari 2011, *IJLCD* 46(5) (n = 29, 10 with aphasia); Northcott et al. follow-up (City Univ.) | MED-HIGH | "The friends drift away; family stays." Ray's teacher friends |
| B5 | **Return to work: ~28% with post-stroke aphasia vs ~45%** for working-age stroke survivors overall. | Review cited in Frontiers Rehab Sci 2025 | MED | Ray on medical leave; "maybe part-time work" goal |
| B6 | **Hospital patients with communication disability are ~3× more likely to have preventable adverse events** (incl. falls); they struggle to report needs, follow safety instructions and use the call bell. | Bartlett et al. 2008; Hemsley et al. (Newcastle/UTS) | HIGH | Scene B stakes |
| B7 | Health staff **talk to family instead of the patient**, stick to "basic needs" and medical status, and avoid conversation partly from fear of not understanding. In recorded visits **no physician wrote down keywords** and only one used a visual aid, though patients said those helped. | Carragher et al. 2024 ("Aphasia disrupts usual care: 'I'm not mad, I'm not deaf'"); Mayo Clinic patient-centred strategies study | MED-HIGH | Scene B; "Ask me. I understand." |
| B8 | **Caregivers of people with aphasia** have **higher burden and lower well-being** than caregivers of stroke survivors without aphasia; one study: aphasia associated with **severe caregiver depression (OR 3.73)**. | Hacettepe thesis; UPH Medicinus study; Brazilian caregiver-overload study | MED (smaller studies, consistent direction) | Denise as secondary user |
| B9 | **Therapy runs out.** Recovery takes years, but **insurance coverage usually ends first**; a common reason therapy stops is insurance limits. | American Stroke Association "Aphasia therapy when insurance stops"; JLCP article | MED | Ray's therapy ended at month 5; "cue me" practice loop |
| B10 | Strokes under 65 are rising (~37% of US stroke hospitalizations were under 65 in 2010; rates up ~15% for under-65s from 2011–2022). | CDC-derived reporting (via news coverage) | MED-LOW | Why Ray is 58 and was working |

## C. How conversation actually breaks for him

| # | Claim | Source | Conf. | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | First-person (stroke survivor with aphasia): *"My brain is still trying to catch up while the conversation has already moved on… what makes it even harder is when people interrupt, finish my sentences, or speak over me… it can feel like my opportunity to understand and respond has been taken away."* | Stroke Association (UK) online community post, "The Part of Aphasia People Don't See" | MED (forum; page blocked here, quote from search snippet; verify before printing) | **Opening quote option.** Problem #1 in the user's own words |
| C2 | David Dow (person with aphasia): *"One of the hardest parts of having aphasia is getting people to understand I know what I want to say."* Another person with aphasia, 12 years after a stroke, answered the phone and the caller asked *"Where's your mother?"* | American Stroke Association "Communication advice from experts" (people with aphasia) | MED-HIGH (published by ASA) | Problem #2 |
| C3 | *"They talked to each other, and they all talked to Brad about me… It was as if I weren't sitting right there."* | Aphasia blog via aphasia.org / CaringBridge resources | MED | Being talked about, not to |
| C4 | **"Speaking for"**: spouses' overprotection correlates with "speaking for" in conversation, and speaking-for correlates with **less participation by the person with aphasia**. | Croteau & Le Dorze, "Overprotection in conversation in couples with aphasia" (aphasiology.pitt.edu 1550); Oxford CA study of a husband's three speaking-for patterns | MED-HIGH | Partner screen ("Ray has something"): Cue changes partner behaviour |
| C5 | In aphasia conversation, partners and people with aphasia **trade off progressivity (keeping talk moving) against self-repair**, and conversation-level interventions usually favour progressivity. Partners collaboratively complete word searches. | Conversation-analysis work (Laakso; Beeke et al. 2020; AJSLP 2022) | HIGH | Why waiting is hard for partners, and why Cue moves the composing into time they're already talking |
| C6 | People with aphasia rate **phone calls, conversations in noise, and group/stranger talk** as hardest; noise costs them more effort and stress than controls. | BYU noise studies (AJSLP 2023/2024); aphasia needs-assessment tools | MED-HIGH | Sunday dinner = the hard case; phone = out of scope |
| C7 | **Word retrieval is a physiological stressor** in aphasia (heart rate up during naming); time pressure produces aphasia-like naming errors even in healthy adults. | Mayo Clinic preliminary study; "Simulation of aphasic naming performance" (TUM) | MED | Composing during *their* turn removes the on-the-spot pressure |
| C8 | Practitioners advise **10–45 s of wait time** for AAC users; natural turn gaps are ~200 ms; past ~700 ms listeners hear reluctance. | AssistiveWare/AbleNet; Stivers 2009; Kendrick & Torreira 2015; Roberts 2006 | HIGH (science) / MED (practice) | Severity number #3 |

## D. Pointing: why the camera is still worth it (but not the headline)

| # | Claim | Source | Conf. | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | People with aphasia **gesture more than controls**; Broca's aphasia especially uses specific gestures to compensate. Pointing at objects and places is used exactly where a word or grammar is missing. | Sekine & Rose 2013; Klippi 2015; Kong et al. 2015 (TalkBank) | HIGH | Cue builds on what Ray already does |
| D2 | But **identifying what's being pointed at "may need extensive interactional work"** by the partner. | Klippi 2015 (EMCA) | MED-HIGH | Problem #5: the 20-questions game. The camera makes the referent exact |
| D3 | **Chil** (Goodwin): severe non-fluent aphasia, a three-word vocabulary ("yes", "no", "and"), yet "a powerful speaker" because partners built meaning around his pointing, gesture and prosody. | Charles Goodwin, UCLA (1995–2003 papers; "Pointing and the collaborative construction of meaning in aphasia" 2000) | HIGH | Optional story for the poster: competence lives beyond words |
| D4 | People with aphasia's goals: **independence and respect, information, communicating opinions, social/leisure/work participation**. | Worrall et al. 2011, *Aphasiology* (n = 50 interviews) | HIGH | Ray's "what he wants" row; frame Cue around respect and opinions, not speed |

## E. Why Ray abandoned his last device

| # | Claim | Source | Conf. | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | AAC abandonment drivers: **vocabulary that doesn't match daily life**, maintenance/prep burden, stigma of visible tech, cost, complexity, and **not using residual speech/gesture**. One survey (n = 275) reported 67% citing vocabulary mismatch. | Johnson thesis (UNH); Moorcroft et al. 2019 (UCL); summary of n = 275 survey via secondary review | MED (the 67% is secondary; don't put it on the poster without the primary) | "Vocabulary from the room" + ring + voice-first |
| E2 | People may be **reluctant to use AAC in public with unfamiliar partners**. | Same reviews | MED | Discreet ring; private earbud preview |
| E3 | "The words get stuck in my brain. It helps me get them out": people with aphasia valued an aid that helped them build sentences themselves (SentenceShaper). | aphasiology.pitt.edu 2074 (n = 7) | MED | Authorship: Cue helps *his* sentence out, doesn't write one for him |
