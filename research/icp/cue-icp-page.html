Ray isn't slow. He's late, and late gets you talked over.

Cue's ideal customer is one adult with chronic non-fluent aphasia after a stroke, at home with a spouse. This page shows who he is, ranks what hurts, walks through his day, and gives the pitch in his terms.

Research verdict: lead with losing his turn, told through Ray. The camera is act two: it turns the pointing he already does into words.

The one person

Ray, 58

A fictional composite. Every detail matches a documented pattern for this group; say "composite" if asked.

Was
High-school chemistry teacher for 30 years in Ypsilanti. Talked for a living. Bad puns.
Stroke
Left-hemisphere (MCA), 14 months ago. Moderate non-fluent (Broca's-type) aphasia with apraxia of speech.
Home
Wife Denise, 56, works full time. Granddaughter Maya, 9, is over most Sundays.
Care
Outpatient speech therapy ended at month 5 when the visits ran out. Aphasia group once a week.
AAC
A symbol app on an iPad. Used it two weeks; now it's in a drawer. Too many folders, too slow, made him "look sick" at dinner.
Wants
Independence and respect. To give his opinion. Social life back, maybe part-time work. Not "talking faster".

What he can do

  • Understands most everyday conversation
  • Knows exactly what he wants to say
  • 1–3 word bursts: "Coffee… no. Uh… tea."
  • Nouns come easier than verbs
  • Points and gestures constantly
  • "Yes", "no" and a few automatic phrases

What he can't do

  • Build a sentence on demand, on time
  • Read more than a few words without effort
  • Use his right hand well (cane in the left)
  • Phone calls
  • Get a word in at a loud family dinner

The second user: Denise

Caregivers of stroke survivors with aphasia carry more burden and more depression than those without it. She often speaks for Ray: answers the doctor, finishes his sentences. Research links that "speaking for" to Ray taking part less. Cue has to change her behavior too, so the partner screen tells her when to wait.

By design

Ray's worst problem isn't vocabulary. It's losing his turn: the conversation can't wait, so others speak for him, he looks less capable than he is, and friends drift away. Problems 3 and 5 are how Cue lets him compose fast enough to get the turn back.

A day with Ray

Four scenes, before and with Cue

7:45 am · breakfast

"Coffee or tea?"

Before: "Tea." Yes/no and either/or questions already work.

With Cue: No change. Cue stays quiet. If Ray speaks, any queued line is cancelled. His voice beats Cue's voice.

11:30 am · neurologist

"How's his balance?"

Before: The doctor asks Denise. Ray fell Tuesday and she doesn't know. "Uh… Tues…" The doctor moves on.

With Cue: Double-click: "Ask me. I understand." plays instantly. The desk screen says "Ray is composing…". He taps fall + Tuesday, hears options, picks "I fell on Tuesday."

The demo

5:30 pm Sunday · kitchen table

Maya's volcano

Before: "Bak… uh…" His son: "Dad means it's fine, sweetie." Topic moves to dinner. Ray goes quiet.

With Cue: While Maya talks, he points at the baking soda, clicks, taps more, hears "Add more baking soda", holds. Screen: "Ray has something." She pauses mid-sentence; Cue waits. She finishes; Cue speaks. "Grandpa, really? How much?"

8:30 pm · TV

The joke on screen

Before: The moment's gone before he can say anything.

With Cue: One click: "Ha! Did you see that?" in under 150 ms. Faster replies are how people feel connected.

What Cue can't do for Ray: phone calls, reading his mail, long talk about things that aren't in the room. It doesn't treat aphasia; it gets him his turn back.

The core is a pair, not one feature

#1 assumes Ray can compose while still listening, and people with aphasia pay a bigger cost when doing two things at once. So #2 is the other half of the core: compose ahead when he can, hold the floor when he can't. The demo shows both. Rows 7–10 are cut from the 90 seconds.

The obvious questions

Answers a judge can't knock over

Texting

"Why can't he just text?"

Writing and reading break with speech: about 68% of people with chronic aphasia have reading trouble, and agraphia is common after left-side strokes. Some people with aphasia do text, but how well it works tracks severity and reading/writing, and Ray is the group it works worst for. One hand. And a text is head-down and late.

"A text is a message to someone who isn't there. Ray needs his turn in the room."

Private send

"Then why text privately through Cue?"

Honestly, it's a weak feature and it's cut from the pitch. Its only edge over texting: he composes by pointing and one tap, with no spelling, and it reaches Denise without the room hearing.

"Same compose flow, sent to one person, because he can't spell a text. A side door, not the product."

Ring vs tablet

"Is a ring plus a tablet faster than tapping the tablet?"

Per tap, no. For a word already on his board it's about the same; for a word that isn't, much faster (no spelling). The win is when the composing happens (during your turn), eyes up, with his one good hand. Everything also works by tapping.

"We don't measure words per minute. We measure the gap: about 30 seconds with a board, under a second with Cue."

Speech recognition

"Why not Siri or dictation?"

Off-the-shelf speech recognition had about 61–70% word error on aphasic speech, and around 32–36% even after fine-tuning. His problem is getting words out, not transcribing them.

Partner training

"Why not train his family to wait?"

Partner training works, but only for partners who get it. His doctor, the waiter and his old colleagues never will. The "Ray has something" screen is a partner-training prompt that comes with him.

Competition

"Lingraphica and Lucid Voice already do AI replies."

They help with what to say and still speak whenever he presses the button. Cue decides when: at your turn end. We found nothing else that does.

Recovery

"Won't he just recover?"

Many don't: about two-thirds are still aphasic at 12 months, and 30–43% remain severely aphasic at 18 months. Ray is at 14 months.

Software vs hardware

"Isn't the invention just software?"

Yes. The ring exists because hold, react and "wait" happen in under a second while his eyes are on a face. If the ring fails, the software still works from the screen.

The pitch

How to present it

The first 15 seconds

Ray taught chemistry for 30 years. Since his stroke he has aphasia: he understands everything and knows what he wants to say, but the words come one at a time. At Sunday dinner, by the time his answer is ready, his son is answering for him. Ray isn't slow. He's late, and late gets you talked over. Cue gets him his turn back.

Severity in three numbers

2M+Americans live with aphasia, more than Parkinson's, and 86% of people have never heard the word.
62%show depression a year after stroke. Quality of life is rated below cancer. Friends are what disappears.
200 msis a normal turn gap. Ray needs 10–45 s. Past 700 ms, a "yes" starts to sound reluctant.

Hard questions

"Can he even use this?"

His profile, non-fluent aphasia with good comprehension and one good hand, is the one that uses AAC independently. We designed for his left hand and limited reading, and we name who it won't work for.

"Is the AI putting words in his mouth?"

Ray picks every word, and the literal option is always first. What he hands to Cue is when, not what.

"He abandoned his iPad app. Why use this?"

He dropped it for the usual reasons: wrong vocabulary, slow, eyes off faces. Cue's vocabulary is what's on the table, it sits on his finger, and his own voice comes first.

"Did you test with people with aphasia?"

Not yet. Ray is a composite from the research. Our first call is the University of Michigan Aphasia Program here in Ann Arbor, the oldest in the country.
